## The argument, start to finish

Every slide is a link in this chain. If a slide isn't on the chain, cut it.

1. Small contractors lose time and money because recording reality costs attention.
2. conTRACKtor makes recording reality nearly effortless.
3. AI interprets what the contractor says and photographs; deterministic software turns it into trustworthy business records.
4. Those records tell him what happened, what needs doing, where his money went, and whether the job made money.
5. Result: less administrative drag, less value slipping through the cracks.

Top-line message, used on slide 1 and again to close:

> **You do the work. conTRACKtor does the paperwork.**

## Slide 7 — What the contractor actually does

Three ways to give conTRACKtor reality. That's the entire user contribution.

**Capture Receipt** — open, camera, shoot. "Receipt secured." Phone goes back in the pocket. Extraction, reconciliation, job matching and expense creation are the software's problem.

**Tell conTRACKtor** — normal language. *"Joe and I worked 8 hours on Benson and we need another box of deck screws."* One sentence becomes hours and a shopping need, both traceable back to the original words.

**Start Work** — a timer, when the work is happening now. Manual hours as fallback.

All three feed one job history and one financial model.

**Note:** this slide wants a real screenshot or a 10-second screen recording. If you're presenting live, demo it instead of showing it.

## Slide 8 — What he gets back

The contractor gives conTRACKtor reality. conTRACKtor answers four questions:

> **What happened?**
> **What needs to happen?**
> **Where did the money go?**
> **Am I making money?**

That is close to the entire application.

And the fifth question, which is where Pro gets genuinely interesting:

> **What am I forgetting?**

**Worth saying out loud:** the product deliberately refuses to guess. On time-and-materials work, customer balance stays unknown until invoicing — the app will not invent it from unbilled labor. That restraint is what makes the number trustworthy, and naming it is more persuasive than another feature.

## Slide 9 — A product constitution

conTRACKtor is governed by an explicit written constitution covering UX, AI behavior, automation authority, and financial integrity. Every workflow change is evaluated against it before it's coded.

**The claim to make — not the rule count:**

> It exists to prevent feature creep and preserve low-attention workflows as the product grows.

Show three or four rules, no more:

- **Capture first, classify later.** Don't make him pick a category before he can photograph a receipt.
- **Forms are fallback UI.** Necessary for corrections and edge cases. Not the primary interaction model.
- **Review is an exception state.** Not part of every happy path — *and not required merely because AI was involved.*
- **Every tap must earn its place.** "The data model is cleaner this way" is not a UX justification.

**Why this matters in your pitch specifically:** the advantage here isn't a patent or a proprietary dataset. A real part of the thesis is *judgment about how contractor software should work*. Any competent team can ship these features. Almost none will hold the line on not adding the screen, the confirmation, the required field.

## Slide 10 — AI interprets. Humans approve. Code commits.

Your strongest technical slide. State the near-term model exactly as the product behaves today:

> **AI interprets. Humans approve. Code commits.**
>
> **AI does not get the database. AI gets conTRACKtor capabilities.**

Concretely:

- The model reads receipts, parses sentences, proposes destinations, drafts descriptions.
- It proposes. It does not commit. Tell conTRACKtor shows *"Here is what I got"*, the human approves, and a single atomic idempotent server capability writes the records. Undo is available afterward.
- Deterministic code owns totals, tax, discounts, reconciliation, permissions, idempotency, and every write.
- AI never receives raw database authority — only permission-aware, business-scoped capabilities, validated for auth, role, consequence and idempotency before anything commits.
- Original source is preserved separately from AI-derived structure. Every automated action is traceable: who initiated it, who or what performed it, what was inferred.

**The maturity ladder — a small two-row diagram, not a three-tier table:**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Today** | AI interprets → proposes → human approves → deterministic capability commits |
| **Future** | Low-risk, well-proven actions graduate to *Saved · Undo*. High-consequence actions — charging a customer, refunds, destructive edits — never execute automatically, at any maturity level. |

**Why this framing is stronger than "the AI does it automatically":** it says the architecture supports graduated autonomy and that you've chosen not to use it yet. That reads as maturity. And AI is still doing enormous work — one spoken sentence becoming hours, notes and shopping needs — even though the human taps Approve.

**The line for anyone technical:** *"Automation confidence is grounded in deterministic reconciliation and evidence, not in the model's self-report."*

## Slide 12 — Business model

> **Free preserves and explains reality.**
> **Pro works on that reality for the contractor.**
> **Business lets the whole crew contribute to it.**

**Free** — everything required for the records to be *true*: jobs, hours, receipts and extraction, expenses, payments, notes, invoices, exports, job history, shopping needs, basic Snapshot, basic Tell.

**Pro — $19/month or $190/year (working hypothesis)** — conTRACKtor doing work on the contractor's behalf: higher AI usage, deeper Tell, suggestion and interpretation, cross-record reasoning, unbilled-work detection, proactive warnings.

**Business** — roles and permissions, crew capture, owner oversight, business-wide accountability.

**Note on Business, and it's a bigger deal than a feature list suggests:** a crew member with his own login, his own assigned jobs, his own hours, receipts, notes and tasks changes conTRACKtor from a solo tool into a multi-person operating system for the business. That's a real tier boundary, not a packaging decision.

**Note on Pro:** keep the *principle* on the slide and resist listing which specific capabilities land in Pro. Entitlements are resolved at runtime and features are meant to move between plans as you learn. Locking "Tell task matching = Pro" into a deck commits you publicly to a decision you deliberately built the flexibility to defer.

**Two things to say out loud, because they're the strategy:**

1. **Correctness is never paywalled.** Reconciling discounts, tax, rebates and required attention stays in Free even though AI helps produce it. You don't sell accuracy — you sell the software doing work on his behalf.
2. **Free is a protected baseline, enforced in code.** A Pro outage cannot break a Free workflow, and a downgrade never hides historical records. Written operational rule, not a promise.

## Slide 13 — Market

**Define the customer operationally before touching a classification code.** Proposed definition:

> US contracting businesses where the owner is still directly involved in field work, and the organization is small enough that administrative attention is the binding constraint.

*Then* map industry classifications to that definition — not the other way round. NAICS 238 (Specialty Trade Contractors) is a good place to start, but it isn't the target: small remodelers and general contractors sit in NAICS 236, and handyman and property-maintenance operations sit elsewhere again. **Don't let Census taxonomy accidentally define the product.**

`[NEED]` **Do not put an unsourced TAM on this slide.** With a strategic advisor, a fabricated market number costs more than a missing one. What to pull:

- **Establishment counts** across the classifications that match the definition above, filtered by employee-size band.
- **Willingness to pay** — what QuickBooks, Buildertrend, JobTread, Housecall Pro and Jobber cost today. Pro at $19/month against those is a strong slide by itself.
- **Bottom-up sizing:** *(qualifying establishments) × (plausible penetration) × ($228/year)*. Bottom-up survives scrutiny; "$X billion market, we need 1%" does not.

## Slide 14 — Where it goes

**Now (MVP gate):** run one real job end to end → fix only the friction that run exposes → declare the acceptance loop complete.

**Next:** first-class voice into the same Tell pipeline · shopping needs as evidence for receipt allocation · smarter multi-destination receipt splits · fuel and vehicle receipt intelligence.

**Then:** proactive automation — unbilled work detected, final invoice prepared when a job closes with a balance, overdue reminder drafted before you notice. This is where *"What am I forgetting?"* gets answered.

**Later:** crew accounts, online payments, integrations.

**The framing that matters:** voice, proactive automation and broader assistant behavior all wait until one real contractor has run one real job start to finish. State that explicitly. Discipline about *sequence* is a large part of what an advisor is actually evaluating.

## Notes on the build

**Length.** Sixteen slides suits a live conversation with an advisor. To send cold, cut to 10: fold 4 into 3, drop 5, fold 9 into 10, drop 14.

**Sequencing choice worth defending.** Slides 9 and 10 sit unusually early for a pitch deck. That's deliberate for this audience — an advisor is evaluating judgment, not just opportunity. If the deck is later reused for investors, both move after traction.

**What this deck deliberately doesn't do:** claim to replace QuickBooks, promise AI autonomy over money, or show a hockey stick. All three would undercut the thing that makes the pitch land.
